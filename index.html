02Brand & Business Overview

What Midrar Does — and Why It Matters

Before analyzing content, it is essential to understand the full scope of Midrar's business model and its position within Saudi Arabia's rapidly evolving proptech landscape.

Business Model Summary

Midrar (مدرار) operates as a real estate financial technology platform based in Saudi Arabia, operating under the Midrar Proptech umbrella. Its product portfolio sits at the intersection of real estate investment and rental payment infrastructure — a relatively rare combination in the MENA proptech space.

The platform serves three distinct user groups simultaneously: property owners who want to collect rent upfront without waiting for monthly payments; tenants who need flexible installment-based payment options; and investors seeking fractional entry into income-generating real estate without full property acquisition.

Core Products

  • Fractional Ownership (التملك الجزئي): Enables investors to own a documented, registered share (نسبة مشاعة) in income-generating properties, with returns proportional to ownership percentage.
  • Usufruct Rights (حق الانتفاع): A Shariah-compliant investment structure allowing investors to benefit from operational returns of a property for a defined period without transferring legal ownership.
  • Landlord Financing: Midrar advances the full annual rent to property owners upfront, eliminating collection risk and cash flow uncertainty.
  • Tenant Installments: Tenants pay their rent in flexible monthly installments rather than a single lump-sum cheque, reducing financial strain.

Market Context

Saudi Arabia's proptech sector is experiencing rapid growth driven by Vision 2030's housing and investment goals, with REGA actively building a regulatory sandbox for fractional ownership platforms. The first REGA sandbox cohort supported nine platforms, all of which have since received official operating licenses — signaling a maturing, increasingly competitive landscape.

Key competitors in adjacent spaces include Ghanem (fractional ownership, USD 7.1M funded), Jozo (tokenized real estate, $2.2M seed), and Mdak (crowdfunded fractional investment). Midrar's unique angle is the dual-sided rental financing component alongside investment products — a differentiation that the current website fails to communicate effectively.

Strategic Tension: Midrar is trying to serve two fundamentally different user mindsets on the same homepage — people managing cash flow (landlords/tenants) and people growing wealth (investors). This creates a content architecture challenge that the current site has not solved.

04Content Strengths

What the Website Gets Right

Recognizing genuine strengths is as important as diagnosing gaps. These are the content decisions Midrar has made that work — and should be amplified, not replaced.

✓ Content Strengths

Clear Product Differentiation in Copy

The homepage copy correctly distinguishes between the landlord upfront model and the tenant installment model — two fundamentally different problems solved by one product. This is logically structured even if not visually clear.

Arabic-First Language Strategy

Midrar leads in Arabic, which is appropriate for a Saudi market targeting landlords and tenants who are not necessarily digital-finance savvy. The decision to lead in the native language reduces friction significantly compared to English-first competitors.

FAQ Section Addresses Real Objections

The FAQ (frequently asked questions) addresses some of the product mechanics — particularly for fractional ownership and usufruct rights — in a plain-language format. This reduces confusion for middle-funnel visitors and partially substitutes for the missing explainer content.

Proptech Framing Aligns with Vision 2030

Positioning Midrar as a "proptech" company rather than a lending or finance company is strategically correct. It aligns with REGA's sandbox narrative and frames the brand as a technology innovator rather than a financial intermediary — reducing potential regulatory-skepticism from visitors.

Shariah Compliance Signals Are Present

The website references Shariah-compliant structures for its products. In a market where Islamic finance is not optional but expected, this is table stakes — and Midrar has at least acknowledged it, unlike some competitors who bury it in footnotes.

✗ Strengths That Are Undermined

Product Clarity — But No Visual Hierarchy

The copy explains the products, but the visual weight on the page doesn't guide the reader to the right product for their situation. A landlord and a tenant both see the same undifferentiated block of text.

Arabic Language — But Inconsistent Bilingual Experience

While Arabic is the primary language, the English version of the site appears underdeveloped or missing entirely. For a proptech brand attracting institutional partners, expat landlords, or GCC investors comfortable in English, this creates a trust gap.

FAQ Exists — But Is Generic and Incomplete

The FAQ answers generic mechanics but doesn't address the emotional objections that actually prevent conversion: "What if my tenant refuses to participate?" or "Is my capital protected?" These are the questions that matter most at the decision point.

Proptech Positioning — But No Tech Evidence

Calling the platform proptech without showing what the technology actually does — no dashboard previews, no app screenshots, no process visualization — makes the claim feel like a label rather than a proof point.

Shariah Compliance Mentioned — But Not Documented

Mentioning Shariah compliance without linking to a Sharia committee certificate, a fatwa, or any official documentation is significantly weaker than what competitors like Ghanem publish — full committee decisions in PDF, named scholars, and decision numbers.

05Weaknesses & Content Gaps

The Gaps That Are Costing Conversions

These are not cosmetic issues. Each gap identified below represents a point in the user journey where a potential customer loses confidence and leaves — often without returning.

Category A: Messaging & Clarity Gaps

  • No clear primary headline that states the core promise. The hero section opens with a tagline about "innovative proptech solutions" but does not tell a landlord, a tenant, or an investor what specifically changes for them in 10 words or less. The value proposition is abstract where it should be specific.
  • Product terminology is assumed, not explained. Terms like "حق الانتفاع" (usufruct rights) appear without immediate plain-language definition on first encounter. A first-time visitor from Google has no context for this term and will not scroll past it.
  • No product comparison format. There is no matrix or visual comparison showing the difference between fractional ownership and usufruct rights — two products that are conceptually close enough to confuse an average investor without detailed financial literacy.
  • The two sides of the rental product (landlord + tenant) are not addressed as a connected system. A landlord who reads the tenant section might wonder whether tenants are paying a premium that hurts the owner's relationship with them. This is never addressed.

Category B: Trust & Social Proof Gaps

  • Zero testimonials or customer stories. Not a single quote, case study, or user outcome appears on the site. For a financial product asking users to trust Midrar with real estate transactions worth tens of thousands of riyals, this is a critical conversion barrier.
  • No transaction or platform metrics. Competitors show funded deal values, number of investors, and total rent processed. Midrar shows nothing. Even early-stage numbers (SAR 2M processed, 150 landlords served) would dramatically shift perceived credibility.
  • No team or founders page. In financial services, trust flows from people. Institutional and retail investors both want to know who is running this platform. The complete absence of a team page is a significant credibility gap.
  • Regulatory status is unclear. Whether Midrar operates under REGA's sandbox, SAMA licensing, or another regulatory framework is never stated explicitly on the site. This is a deal-breaker for cautious investors who know the Saudi financial services landscape.

Category C: Conversion Architecture Gaps

  • No dedicated landing pages per product or audience. A landlord looking to collect rent upfront and an investor looking to buy fractional shares both land on the same homepage and are expected to self-identify. There is no "Are you a landlord? Click here" UX pattern.
  • CTA buttons are generic and low-intent. The dominant call-to-action on the site appears to be a general "contact us" or "learn more" pattern — neither of which creates urgency or speaks to a specific outcome. High-converting financial CTAs should be product-specific: "See available properties," "Get your advance rent offer," "Start with SAR 1,000."
  • No lead capture mechanism beyond generic contact. There is no newsletter, no waitlist for new properties, no downloadable guide, and no structured form that qualifies the lead type. All acquisition is forced through the same funnel regardless of user intent.

Category D: Content Depth Gaps

  • No blog, no articles, no educational content. This is perhaps the largest single gap. The Saudi proptech space is rich with unanswered questions that everyday users are searching for — "كيف أستثمر في العقار بمبلغ صغير," "حق الانتفاع مقابل التملك الجزئي" — and Midrar is not capturing any of that organic intent.
  • No video content or product walkthrough. Financial products with complex structures (fractional ownership, usufruct, rental financing) convert significantly better when demonstrated visually. A 90-second explainer video per product would reduce FAQ volume and increase conversion.
  • No case study or deal showcase. Showing a completed deal — property type, location, return achieved, investor profile — is the single most powerful trust-building content format for a real estate investment platform. It is entirely absent.

The Pattern: Midrar's content weaknesses cluster around one theme — the platform asks users to trust it with major financial decisions while providing almost none of the proof, transparency, or specificity that trust requires. Every category of weakness above is, at its root, a trust problem disguised as a content problem.

06Messaging & Positioning Analysis

Is the Message Reaching the Right Person?

Beyond what the website says, this section examines how it says it — and whether the messaging hierarchy, tone, and competitive differentiation are working in Midrar's favor.

Current Value Proposition Audit

Midrar's current implied value proposition reads as something like: "We offer innovative proptech solutions through fractional ownership, usufruct rights, and flexible rental payments."

The problem is that this is a feature list, not a promise. A value proposition must answer "What changes in your life because of us?" — not "What do we offer?"

Compare this to how the strongest proptech value propositions are structured:

  • For Landlords: "Collect your full year's rent today. Midrar handles the rest." — specific, outcome-driven, removes a known pain point.
  • For Tenants: "Split your rent into easy monthly payments. No full cheque, no stress." — tangible, emotionally resonant, addresses a real financial anxiety.
  • For Investors: "Own a registered share in income-generating property from SAR 1,000. Earn monthly returns." — low barrier, documented proof, clear reward.

None of these propositions exist on the current site in this clarity or specificity. The messaging is positioned around Midrar as a company, rather than around the user's problem and outcome.

Tone & Voice Assessment

The current tone sits somewhere between formal financial Arabic and corporate tech language. This hybrid is not wrong, but it lacks distinctiveness. The copy does not feel like it belongs to a company with a specific personality — it reads like a category description rather than a brand voice.

For a fintech brand operating in a trust-sensitive financial category, the recommended tone profile is:

  • Confident but approachable — authoritative on how the product works, without being inaccessible to a first-time user.
  • Transparent — proactively addresses fees, risks, and regulatory status before users ask.
  • Outcome-focused — leads with what users gain, not what Midrar provides.
  • Human — a real customer's problem described in their own words, not in legal/financial language.

Voice Recommendation: Midrar should develop a distinct content voice guide — defining what the brand says, what it never says, and how it talks about money, risk, and property. This consistency would pay dividends across website, social, and customer communications.

On-Page SEO Issues to Address

  • Heading Hierarchy (H1/H2/H3): A single-page site or minimal structure often uses visual headings that are not semantically tagged as H1–H3. Search engines rely on this structure to understand page content. Each product section should have a keyword-relevant H2.
  • Meta Descriptions: Without visibility into the site's HTML, it is highly likely that meta descriptions are generic or auto-generated. Each page should have a unique, compelling meta description under 155 characters that includes a target keyword.
  • FAQ Schema Markup: The existing FAQ section is valuable but likely not marked up with structured data (schema.org/FAQPage). Adding this enables rich result snippets in Google — directly increasing click-through rates without any ranking change.
  • Image Alt Text: Any images or icons on the site should have descriptive alt text including relevant Arabic or English keywords — both for SEO and accessibility compliance.
  • Internal Linking: A single-page or low-content site has no internal linking structure to distribute authority. As content is added (blog, landing pages), a deliberate internal linking strategy is critical.
  • Page Speed and Core Web Vitals: Arabic RTL sites with animations or heavy assets can struggle with LCP (Largest Contentful Paint) metrics. A technical audit is recommended to ensure the site meets Google's Core Web Vitals thresholds.
  • Canonical Tags and Bilingual Hreflang: If Midrar has or plans an English version of the site, proper hreflang tags must be implemented to prevent duplicate content penalties and ensure the right language version ranks for the right audience.

    Build a Case Study / Deal Showcase Library

    Document 3–5 completed deals or customer stories — one landlord story, one tenant story, one fractional investor story. Each case study should follow a structure: situation, problem, solution, specific outcome (in SAR if possible). This content format drives the highest purchase intent of any format in financial services and almost no proptech platform in Saudi Arabia is doing this at any quality level. Even anonymized stories with pseudonyms are significantly more effective than no social proof.

    Medium Effort High Impact
  • S6

    Develop a Bilingual Content Strategy (Arabic + English)

    Build a structured approach to bilingual content: Arabic as the primary audience-facing language for landlords, tenants, and Saudi retail investors; English as the secondary language for institutional partners, GCC expat investors, and international media. This does not require translating every page — it requires a deliberate decision about which pages matter in English and building those pages properly, rather than having a half-built English version that undermines brand credibility.

    Higher Effort High Impact
  • S7

    Establish a Press / Media Page and Start Seeking Coverage

    Create a press page with the company logo in high resolution, key facts, executive headshots, and a press contact. Actively pitch to Arab Business, Al Arabiya, Mubasher, and English-language fintech media. Each piece of press coverage creates a backlink, a trust signal, and a potential investor/partner referral. The "As seen in" logos on a homepage are worth more in conversion uplift than almost any other single trust element.

    Medium Effort High Impact
